The climb feels earned

How NBA 2K27’s MyCAREER makes meritocratic individualism feel fair, and what its rating leaves out.

Scroll to raise the rating

At the start of NBA 2K27’s MyCAREER, a 60 overall rating makes a future NBA star look ordinary. The number appears before the player has earned a place in the league, and it turns potential into a visible gap between who the MyPLAYER is and who they could become. MyCAREER presents that gap as a challenge the player can close through effort. Its worldview is meritocratic individualism: a person can rise by developing individual skill and proving their worth through competition. Yet the climb also depends on how the game structures opportunity, how players spend money, and whether they cooperate. MyCAREER makes earned improvement feel fair, while showing that merit is never the only force shaping progress.

The overall rating gives this idea a simple visual form. One number appears to summarize a player’s ability, making progress easy to recognize: a higher rating means a stronger player. Starting at 60 creates an apparent common baseline. The player isn’t a superstar who begins with everything; they have room to improve. That can make later progress feel deserved, since the rating changes after time spent playing, practicing, and making decisions about a build. The meter turns development into something measurable. It also encourages the player to see a low rating as an individual problem to solve: work on the build, improve its weaknesses, and earn a higher number.

The game’s progression systems reinforce that message. 2K describes Badge Token progress as tied to in-game performance, Team Practice drills, and Gatorade workouts. In the modern MyCAREER story, a player’s performance can also affect their draft position (“NBA 2K27 MyPLAYER Builder”; “NBA 2K27 MyCAREER”). These systems connect action to reward. Practice and competition produce visible progress, so the game suggests that persistence and skill can change a player’s status. The result is an appealing kind of control: improvement feels connected to the player’s choices instead of luck. When a rating or badge improves, the game offers a clear explanation for why the player deserves it.

Research helps separate the ideal of merit from the claim that real systems always reward merit fairly. Leanne Son Hing and her coauthors distinguish between believing that outcomes should be based on merit and believing that the world does distribute outcomes that way. The second belief, they write, “can serve as a hierarchy-legitimizing ideology that justifies current societal inequality” (448). MyCAREER makes the first idea attractive: players should improve through practice and prove themselves on the court. But it can also make the second idea feel natural. If a stronger build has a higher number, it is easy to assume that the player with the higher rating must have earned that status under fair conditions.

The VC system complicates that assumption. 2K Support explains that players can earn VC through game modes and spend it on MyPLAYER attributes; it also says they can buy VC with real money to get it faster (“VC and MTP”). That means the path from 60 overall to a stronger player can involve both gameplay and spending. Money doesn’t replace skill in competition, but it can change how quickly a player develops. The game’s meritocratic story therefore has a condition: effort matters, yet the resources available to the player may affect the pace of improvement. The starting rating suggests an open contest, while the upgrade economy offers another way to move ahead.

Jonathan Mijs’s research gives this tension a broader context. In his study of meritocracy in education, he argues that “opportunities for merit are themselves determined by non-meritocratic factors” (14). His article is about education, not video games, so it can’t prove what MyCAREER does to its players. It offers a lens for asking who gets the chance to demonstrate merit. In the game, the build menu sets limits and categories for development, while VC and time shape how quickly a player can use them. The player makes choices, but those choices operate inside rules created by the developers. A high rating is evidence of progress within that system; it doesn’t show that every player had identical resources or faced the same path.

An article by Anatolia Batruch and her coauthors suggests why this distinction matters beyond a game. Their studies found that belief in school meritocracy “reduces the perceived unfairness of social class inequality in society” (621). That finding concerns education and social inequality, not NBA 2K. Still, it raises a useful question about MyCAREER: when a game repeatedly links status to measurable achievement, what becomes easy to overlook? The mode can make the player focus on their own performance and upgrades, while leaving the rules that define success in the background. I don’t take that as proof that playing the game causes a belief about real life. I read it as a familiar story about achievement: if outcomes look measurable, they can seem fair.

There is a counterargument: MyCAREER is a basketball game, not a complete model of society. A progression system gives players goals, and practice should make a virtual athlete better. That is part of the fun. The mode also includes cooperative play; 2K says a friend can join a career through Running Mate Trace Miller, with both players earning VC and Season XP (“NBA 2K27 MyCAREER”). That feature complicates the idea that success belongs to one individual alone. Basketball depends on teammates, and the game makes room for another person to share the climb. Individual progress remains the focus, but cooperation can help produce it.

MyCAREER’s worldview matters because its numbers make improvement satisfying and easy to read. Moving beyond 60 overall can feel like proof that effort pays off. That is a motivating idea, and the game gives players real agency over how they develop. At the same time, the upgrade economy and the structure of the build show that effort takes place within limits and resources the player didn’t create. I see the mode as both a celebration of self-improvement and a reminder to question what a rating leaves out.

The climb feels earned; the rules of the climb deserve attention too.
